\section{Introduction}
Visual art creation is a complex, multistage process requiring significant time, effort, and material investment. 
Rapid advances in visual generative models (\textit{e.g.,} general-purpose, zero-shot \cite{paradox, lgtm}) and creativity support tools attempt to assist artists through more consistent, guided generation and editing \cite{zhang2023text} of increasingly realistic and detailed outputs \cite{podell2024sdxl} -- sparking excitement around the potential to (1) augment the early ideation workflows of professional creatives \cite{wang2025aideation, GenQuery, architectural, art_implications, poet, lamuse} and (2) revolutionize and democratize art education and participation \cite{democratize, edu}. 
While such tools offer promise for helping people develop skills, enjoy the process of art making, and gain a deeper appreciation for visual craft \cite{jackson}, in practice they often cause stress and disorientation \cite{kawakami2024impact, lgtm} when creators attempt to integrate emerging tools into their creative processes \cite{unstraighten} and leave an AI-graveyard of failures \cite{graveyard} at significant environmental costs \cite{hungry}. Artists also remain deeply distrusting of generative systems due to harmful downstream impacts of inadequate attribution and compensation on their community members, raising questions about what artist-centered, ethically sound uses and developments of generative AI tools could look like.

Currently, model developers often overlook \textit{personal preferences} (as opposed to career or professional objectives) of individual artists as well as their sociomaterial realities. In particular, \textit{\textbf{non-working artists}} (\textit{e.g.,} novices and casual creators who engage in artmaking for personal objectives instead of making a living) often engage in art-making to explore new capabilities \cite{clinical, personal}, practice self-expression \cite{real_benefits} or challenge conventional aesthetic and creative boundaries \cite{decline_novelty, art_implications}. Additionally, much of the existing literature around creativity support focused on final-stage outputs of \textit{professional} designers and artists \cite{design_professionals, shi2023understanding} --- leaving the early stages workflows of novice visual artists underexplored.
In particular, few studies examined how non-working or novice artists \cite{hu2025designing} adopted co-creation and inspiration tools to meaningfully organize and reflect on their existing resources \cite{lamuse}, leaving questions around how emerging generative capabilities may align with their \textbf{early-stage workflows} --  \textit{i.e.} practices that start at the definition of a piece and ending with the use of references for revisions to pieces  \cite{botella2018stages}.

To address these gaps, this study blends qualitative, ethnographic approaches of inquiry with the expert understandings of model developers to co-design more meaningful and effective ways of supporting non-working visual artists with generative AI tools in their early-stage workflows. 
% While prior work focused on AI-generated art \cite{explorers, machine} or final-image generation \cite{lgtm}, 
We employed a two-step approach that (1) interviewed 15 novice artists to understand their existing practices, initial perceptions of available generative tools, as well as painpoints to their artistic creation processes (\S\ref{interview}); (2) mapped the expressed needs to state-of-the-art technical capabilities and develop six functional prototypes, which we evaluated in co-design workshops with 14 novice artists (\S\ref{codesign}).
Leveraging state-of-the-art capabilities (e.g., medium and style transfer, visual combinations and editing, step-by-step tutorials, and extensions of 2D into interactive 3D viewing),
these interactive prototypes allowed us to probe novice artists for reactions and design objectives they prioritized for future forms of tooling support.

Building on the above questions, we map early-stage practice, probe emerging capabilities through co-design and prototypes, and distill implications for toolmakers. Accordingly, this paper contributes.
\begin{itemize}
     \item An empirical account of how novice artists use tools in the early stage and the challenges they face.
    \item Empirically grounded capability requirements for early stage support that artists want in tools.
    \item A synthesis of the tensions these tools raise and the implications for design and policy.
